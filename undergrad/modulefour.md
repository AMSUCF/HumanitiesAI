<span class="unit-badge unit-badge--images">Images</span>

We now move from words to images. Image generators are trained on enormous collections of pictures and captions gathered from the web, and they learn to associate words with visual patterns. That means that when you type "a professor" or "a house in Mexico," the image you get back reflects what was common in that training data, and the choices of the people who built and filtered it.

This module's readings give you tools for looking critically at those results. In "The Power Chapter" from *Data Feminism*, Catherine D'Ignazio and Lauren Klein ask us to *examine power*: to ask who makes data systems, who they serve, and whose perspectives are missing from them. Victoria Turk's "How AI reduces the world to stereotypes," from *Rest of World*, puts that question to the test with thousands of generated images of people and places from around the world. Ian Demsky's "My Month with Midjourney" offers a model for the exercise itself: a writer experimenting with an image generator day after day and paying close attention to what came back.

## Tutorial: Prompting Images

This exercise gets everyone on the same page about what image generation can do right now, and what it reveals about the data behind it. Follow Demsky's approach: work iteratively, keep notes, and pay attention to results that surprise you.

### Choosing a Tool

All of these will work for this exercise:

- [Google Gemini](https://gemini.google.com/) generates images with a free Google account.
- [Microsoft Copilot](https://cdl.ucf.edu/faculty-multimedia-center-ai-tools/), through your UCF account, includes image generation.
- [ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com/) generates images on its free tier, with daily limits.

If you can, try at least one of your prompts in two different tools and compare. In a chat-based tool, **start a new chat for each new prompt**, or the tool will keep modifying your previous image instead of starting fresh.

### Four Prompts

Work through each of these four types of prompt, adapting them to your own interests. Generate several images for each, and revise your prompt at least once.

1. **A general identity.** Ask for an image of a broad role, such as "a college student," "an English major," "a nurse," or "a video game designer." Look closely at the choices the model makes about race, gender, age, clothing, and setting. Then try the "make it more" trick ("make it more English major") and see which details the model treats as defining that identity.
2. **Adding identity markers.** Add an identity marker to the same prompt (for example, "a woman English major" or "an older college student") and notice what changes and what stays the same. In the example below, the course designer's results for "professor of digital culture" and "woman professor of digital culture" show nearly everyone in the same glasses, with very similar ages, skin tones, and expressions.
3. **A place.** Following Turk's article, ask for "a house in [a country]," "a street in [a city]," or "a family dinner in [a country]." If you have a personal connection to a place, use it, since you'll be best able to judge the result. How does the image compare with what you know?
4. **Copyright boundaries.** Ask for something based on a copyrighted character or property (for example, "a college student as a character in *Spider-Verse*"). Depending on the tool, this may be blocked. Then try a generic version ("a college student as a comic book superhero") and compare.

![Midjourney output in two rows of four photorealistic portraits: "woman professor of digital culture" above and "professor of digital culture" below, all middle-aged, nearly all white, and all wearing similar dark-framed glasses](https://anastasiasalter.net/HumanitiesAI/images/midjourney-professors.png)

For each prompt, keep brief notes on your first attempt, your revisions, and anything unexpected.

### Discussion

Share at least one image for each of the four prompt types, along with the tool you used and the exact prompts (including revisions). If a prompt was blocked, share what happened instead: the refusal is a result too. Then reflect:

- Which of your images surprised you most, and why?
- Using D'Ignazio and Klein's idea of examining power, whose view of the world do your images reflect? Who or what was missing?
- Did your "place" images reproduce the kinds of stereotypes Turk describes? If you compared two tools, did they differ?
- Demsky spent a month with one tool. What did you learn from revising your prompts that you wouldn't have learned from a single attempt?

Make sure to reference at least two of the module's readings. Your post is worth 8 points; then reply to two classmates (1 point per reply) for full credit.
