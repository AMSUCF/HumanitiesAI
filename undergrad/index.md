## Course Description

*Catalog description:* Fundamentals of digital humanities approaches to artificial intelligence.

Generative AI tools such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini are changing how we read, write, research, make images, and build things. Digital humanities (DH), the field that brings computational methods to the study of literature, history, art, and culture, has been asking questions about these kinds of tools for decades: what can a computer "read," what goes into an archive and what's left out, and what we learn by building something ourselves. This course uses those foundational DH questions to look at today's AI. Students learn about the history of these technologies and how they are reshaping culture and creativity, and get hands-on practice that shows what these tools can and can't do for literary, cultural, and textual work.

The course is organized into seven two-week modules across three units:

-   **Words.** We start with text: the history of chatbots from ELIZA to today, generating and fact-checking writing, and "distant reading," the DH practice of using computers to read many texts at once.
-   **Images.** We turn to image generation and to digital archives, asking how AI represents people and places, and what it means when machines describe, sort, and remix cultural collections.
-   **Code.** In a short unit on making, we build a small interactive web project with an AI assistant. No programming experience is required.

The semester closes with a module on the values of digital humanities and the future of AI in the humanities, followed by a final portfolio and reflection.

Each module runs for two weeks and has three parts:

-   **Readings and Lecture.** Short, accessible readings (articles, essays, and one podcast) and a recorded lecture, all available in the module.
-   **Exercise.** A hands-on activity following a step-by-step tutorial, using free or low-cost AI tools.
-   **Discussion.** Share what you made and what you noticed, then respond to two classmates. The emphasis is not on "success" or "failure," but on paying close attention to the process and what it reveals.

## Course Objectives

By the end of this course, students will be able to:

-   Explain what digital humanities is and how its methods, including text analysis, digital archives, and building, connect to generative AI
-   Describe key moments in the history of AI and chatbots, and connect current debates about AI to earlier debates about technology
-   Use generative AI tools for textual, visual, and simple coding projects in the humanities
-   Evaluate AI-generated text and images for accuracy, bias, and representation, and verify sources and claims
-   Discuss the ethical, labor, and copyright questions raised by AI in creative and academic work
-   Reflect on their own use of AI and make informed choices about when and how to use these tools

## Materials and Texts

There is no textbook to purchase for this course. All required readings are open access online or available through the UCF Library, and links are provided in the schedule below and in each Webcourses module. If you run into a paywall on a news or magazine article (for example, *The Atlantic* or *The New Yorker*), use the UCF Library's databases to access it for free, or reach out to the instructor.

Each module also includes a tutorial with examples and links to the free tools we'll use, such as [Voyant Tools](https://voyant-tools.org/) for text analysis and the [Library of Congress's free-to-use collections](https://www.loc.gov/free-to-use/) for archival images.

## AI Tools and Accounts

You will need an account with at least one general-purpose AI assistant: [ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com/), [Claude](https://claude.ai/), or [Google Gemini](https://gemini.google.com/). **A paid subscription is not required.** Every exercise can be completed with free accounts, and UCF students also have access to [Microsoft Copilot through their UCF account](https://cdl.ucf.edu/faculty-multimedia-center-ai-tools/). Free tiers have usage limits, so plan to work on exercises over several sittings rather than all at once. If you already pay for one of these tools, you're welcome to use it.

Some exercises ask you to compare two tools, so it is helpful to have accounts with at least two of them. Never upload private information (your own or anyone else's) to these tools, and read each exercise's notes on privacy and copyright before you begin.

## Asynchronous Online Course Structure

This course is fully asynchronous and online. There are no scheduled class meetings: you'll complete readings, watch lectures, and participate in discussions on your own schedule within each module's two-week window.

-   **Suggested pacing:** In the first week of each module, complete the readings and lecture and start the exercise. In the second week, finish the exercise and post to the discussion by Thursday, which gives your classmates time to reply, then reply to two classmates by Sunday.
-   All module work is due on the final Sunday of the module at 11:59 PM, and is accepted without penalty for one week after that. Once an assignment closes, late work will not be accepted unless an extension has already been arranged with the instructor.
-   Office hours are available both through Webcourses messages and via Zoom. Zoom is recommended for technical problems, where screen-sharing helps.
-   You will need a reliable internet connection and a computer (not just a phone) to complete the exercises.
-   In the event of an emergency or medical challenge, additional flexibility is available: reach out to the instructor as soon as you can to make a plan.
