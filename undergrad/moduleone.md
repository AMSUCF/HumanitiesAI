Welcome! This course sits where two conversations meet. The first is about **digital humanities** (DH): a field that has spent decades bringing computers to the study of literature, history, art, and culture, by digitizing archives, counting words across thousands of novels, mapping historical data, and building tools and websites. The second is about **generative AI**: chatbots, image generators, and coding assistants that have, in just a few years, become part of how many people read, write, and work.

This module's readings set up both conversations. Matthew Kirschenbaum's "What Is Digital Humanities and What's It Doing in English Departments?" is one of the most widely assigned introductions to the field: notice how often he describes DH as a *community* and a set of *practices*, rather than a single method. The *99% Invisible* episode "The ELIZA Effect" tells the story of ELIZA, a chatbot built at MIT in the 1960s, and of how quickly people began to treat a very simple program as if it understood them. That tendency, now called the "ELIZA effect," is worth keeping in mind all semester.

## Tutorial: Talking to Chatbots, Then and Now

The most common way people use generative AI is the chat window: you type, and it types back. But chatbots are much older than ChatGPT. In this exercise, you'll have three conversations that span sixty years of chatbot history, and pay close attention to how each one makes you feel like someone is listening.

### Before You Start: Set Up Your Accounts

If you haven't already, create a free account with at least one of [ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com/), [Claude](https://claude.ai/), or [Google Gemini](https://gemini.google.com/). You can also sign in to [Microsoft Copilot with your UCF account](https://cdl.ucf.edu/faculty-multimedia-center-ai-tools/). You don't need a paid subscription for this course. We'll be using these tools all semester, so this is a good time to look around the interface: find where your past chats are saved, how to start a new chat, and whether you can choose between different models.

### Conversation One: ELIZA

![A conversation with ELIZA in a retro terminal interface](https://anastasiasalter.net/HumanitiesAI/images/eliza.png)

Spend at least 10 minutes talking with [E.L.I.Z.A. Talking](https://www.masswerk.at/eliza/), Norbert Landsteiner's re-creation of Joseph Weizenbaum's original 1966 program (the one from the podcast). ELIZA plays a therapist who mostly turns your statements back into questions. Try talking to it about your week, about starting this class, or about whether computers can think. Then try to "break" it: what happens when you ask it a factual question, change the subject abruptly, or repeat yourself?

### Conversation Two: A Modern Chatbot

Now open ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, or Copilot and start a new chat. First, ask it to play ELIZA's role, with a prompt like:

> For this conversation, please reply as a person-centered therapist, like the 1960s ELIZA chatbot. Keep your responses short and focus on reflecting my statements back to me as questions.

Spend at least 10 minutes having a similar conversation to the one you had with ELIZA. Then step out of the role-play and ask it directly: "How are you different from ELIZA?" Take screenshots of any moments that surprise you.

### Conversation Three: An AI-Built ELIZA

Finally, try [Future ELIZA](https://anastasiasalter.net/HumanitiesAI/demos/WeekOne/), a chatbot that was itself *made* by an AI: the course designer prompted Claude to build an ELIZA-style bot that responds "like an AI from the future." There's no AI running behind this page, only the pre-written responses Claude generated. Spend a few minutes with it. How does knowing that it was built with a few prompts change how you see it?

As you move between the three, keep notes on:

- How often each one repeats itself, and which words or phrases it relies on
- Moments when it felt like it understood you, and moments when the illusion broke
- What you think each one is actually doing when it responds

### Discussion

Since this is our first module, please start your post with a brief introduction: who you are, your major or area of interest, and any experiences you've had with generative AI so far (in school, at work, or just for fun).

Then share at least two screenshots from your conversations (at least one from ELIZA and one from a modern chatbot) and reflect on the experience:

- Which conversation felt most like talking to a person, and why? Where did the illusion break down?
- The "ELIZA effect" describes our tendency to read understanding into a machine's words. Did you notice it happening to you? Point to a specific moment.
- Kirschenbaum describes digital humanities as a field shaped by people asking humanities questions about technology. Based on this exercise, what is one question about chatbots you'd want a humanities scholar, rather than an engineer, to answer?

Make sure to reference at least one of the module's readings. Your post is worth 8 points; then reply to two classmates (1 point per reply) for full credit.
