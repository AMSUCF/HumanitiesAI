## Think: Week 13 (April 12 - 18)

We started the semester with ELIZA, a chatbot whose personality came entirely from a short script written by its designer. We end by writing scripts of our own, and asking what values they carry.

Lisa Spiro's "'This Is Why We Fight': Defining the Values of the Digital Humanities" argues that DH is best defined not by any single tool or method but by shared values, including **openness**, **collaboration**, **collegiality and connectedness**, **diversity**, and **experimentation**. As you read, ask which of these values you've seen in the AI tools we've used this semester, and which seem to be missing. Ted Underwood's "A more interesting upside of AI" offers a hopeful but careful view of the future: rather than imagining AI as a "super-intelligence," he suggests treating it as a *cultural technology*, like writing or printing, that could change how we explore and remix culture itself.

### Exercise: A Study Companion and Its Values

Every AI assistant you've used this semester follows instructions written by its makers, which shape its tone, what it will and won't do, and what it assumes you want. In this exercise, you'll write your own instructions to create a study companion for a text or topic you care about, and you'll design it around values from Spiro's essay.

#### Step 1: Choose a Text or Topic

Pick something a classmate might need help studying: a public-domain text from [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/) (perhaps one from Module Three), a topic from another class you're taking, or a subject from your major.

#### Step 2: Choose a Tool

Use a feature that lets you give a chatbot standing instructions that apply to every conversation:

- **Gemini:** create a **Gem** (free with a Google account).
- **Claude:** create a **Project** and add custom instructions (and, if you like, upload your text).
- **ChatGPT:** create a project with instructions, or a custom GPT if your account allows.

If none of these are available to you, write your instructions as the first message of a new chat, beginning "For the rest of this conversation, follow these instructions."

#### Step 3: Write Instructions Based on Values

Choose **two** of Spiro's values and write instructions (at least 150 words) that put them into practice. For example:

- **Openness:** "Always tell the student where information comes from, and say clearly when you're not sure."
- **Collaboration:** "Don't hand over answers. Ask the student questions that help them work it out, and build on what they say."
- **Diversity:** "When discussing interpretations, include perspectives from more than one critical tradition or community, and point out whose voices are missing."
- **Experimentation:** "Suggest creative ways to explore the text, like rewriting a scene from another character's point of view."

Also include what your companion should do if a student asks it to write their essay for them. (Your instructions are a design document, not a reflection, so you may draft them with AI's help if you like. Your reflection must be your own writing.)

#### Step 4: Test It

Have three conversations with your study companion, and **screenshot each one**:

1. **Test 1:** a straightforward study question about your text or topic
2. **Test 2:** a question where you deliberately make a mistake, to see whether it corrects you
3. **Test 3:** a request to write your paper or answer your homework for you

Revise your instructions at least once based on what you learn, and test again.
